THE TEMPLATE

Five checks, measurement before deletion.

The worked example below is illustrative — not a real incident. It shows how each section reads when filled in.

SECTION 01

Measure before deleting

Record the volume, the free space, and how fast it is shrinking. A disk filling in minutes is a runaway process; a disk that crept full over months is capacity. The growth rate decides whether you have an afternoon or an emergency.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEVolume at 100%, shrinking 2 GB per hour since 06:00 CT — emergency pace. Free space and growth rate recorded before any cleanup. Not a real incident.
SECTION 02

Find what grew

Rank directories by size, then rank by recent change. The largest directory is often years of legitimate data; the fastest-growing one is your culprit. Compare both lists before concluding anything.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLELargest: a 400 GB database (expected). Fastest-growing: the application heap-dump folder, 180 GB in 6 hours after an out-of-memory loop started writing dumps. Not a real incident.
SECTION 03

Rotation and dump settings

Logs that never rotate and dumps that never expire are configuration failures, not mysteries. Check the rotation policy, whether it is actually running, and the dump retention for the failing service. A rotation job silently failing for weeks fills disks on schedule.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEThe log rotation config existed, but its scheduled job had failed since a credential change 5 weeks earlier; logs grew unbounded in two directories. Not a real incident.
SECTION 04

Free space safely

Recover space in ways that preserve evidence and service: truncate or rotate logs rather than deleting open files, expire dumps by age, extend the volume if it is simply undersized. Note exactly what was removed — the next engineer inherits your deletions.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEFreed 120 GB by expiring heap dumps older than 24 hours and rotating logs; no live data deleted. Actions and byte counts logged in the session. Not a real incident.
SECTION 05

Set the prevention

A disk that filled once will fill again on the same schedule. Prevention is one of three things: an alert threshold with headroom to act, a rotation or retention fix that removes the growth source, or a capacity change with a date. Record which one — not 'monitor closely'.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEPrevention set: alert at 80% with a 7-day trend, dump retention capped at 48 hours, and the rotation job given a failure alert of its own. Not a real incident.

QUESTIONS

Disk-full incidents, answered briefly.

What should I do first when a disk fills up?

Measure before deleting: record the free space and how fast it is shrinking, then find the largest and fastest-growing directories. The growth rate tells you whether this is a runaway process or slow capacity creep, and deleting blind destroys the evidence of which one it was.

How do I find what is consuming the disk?

List directories by size, then by recent growth — the two lists answer different questions. The biggest directory is usually legitimate data; the one that grew in the incident window is the culprit, commonly logs with broken rotation, dump files, or temp data from a looping process.

Is it safe to delete log files to free space?

Usually, with two cautions: deleting a file a process holds open may not free the space until the process restarts — truncating works better — and the logs may be the only evidence of the fault that filled the disk. Rotate or archive first when the service allows it, and record what was removed.
