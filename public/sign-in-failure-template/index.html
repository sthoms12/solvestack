THE TEMPLATE

Five checks, from the error outward.

The worked example below is illustrative — not a real incident. It shows how each section reads when filled in.

SECTION 01

Capture the exact error

Sign-in failures announce their layer if you read them literally. Screenshot the error, record the exact code, and save the correlation or trace ID before anything else — that ID is the key that unlocks the IdP's log entry later. 'It just says access denied' is not evidence.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEError: the user is not assigned to a role for the application; correlation ID recorded from the browser trace at 10:41 CT. Not a real incident.
SECTION 02

Establish the scope

One user, one group, or everyone determines the suspect list. A single user points at account state or role assignment; a group points at policy; everyone points at the app registration or the IdP itself. Test with a known-good account in the same policy scope before theorizing.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEFailures limited to one department group of 22 users; admins and other groups signed in normally. Scope says policy or group assignment, not the IdP. Not a real incident.
SECTION 03

Read the IdP sign-in logs

The IdP already knows why it refused: the sign-in log entry for the correlation ID states the failure reason in plain language — policy evaluation, MFA, assignment, or credential. Work from that reason instead of guessing which conditional-access policy fired.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEIdP log for the trace ID: failure reason 'user not assigned to an application role'; a group rename the previous evening had silently emptied the assignment group. Not a real incident.
SECTION 04

Check the app side

If the IdP clears, inspect the application's half of the handshake: the SAML or OIDC signing certificate expiry, the metadata the app cached, and clock skew between the app server and the IdP. Expired signing certificates are the classic Monday-morning outage.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEApp-side check: signing certificate valid for 11 more months; metadata refreshed last quarter; server clock within 2 seconds of the IdP. App side cleared. Not a real incident.
SECTION 05

Account and policy state

Finish with the unglamorous causes: locked or expired accounts, a forced MFA re-registration, a device-compliance policy that started failing after an OS update. These produce 'SSO is down' tickets that are really one policy doing exactly what it was told.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEThe remaining single-user cases resolved as expired passwords surfaced by the new sign-in flow — a communications issue, not an outage. Not a real incident.

QUESTIONS

Sign-in failures, answered briefly.

What should I check first when SSO sign-in fails?

Capture the exact error text and the correlation or trace ID, then establish scope: one user, one group, or everyone. Those two facts decide where to look next — the IdP sign-in log entry for that trace ID states the failure reason, which is faster than auditing policies by hand.

How do I find the real reason in IdP sign-in logs?

Search the sign-in logs for the correlation ID from the error, or for the user and timestamp. The entry names the failure stage — credential, MFA, conditional-access policy, or application assignment — and usually the specific policy or missing assignment. Work from that stated reason rather than the user's description of the error.

What are the most common causes of sudden sign-in failures?

An expired SAML or OIDC signing certificate, a conditional-access policy change, an emptied or renamed assignment group, IdP service degradation, and clock skew on the app server. Sudden failures after a quiet change window are usually one of the first three.
