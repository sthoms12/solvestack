THE TEMPLATE

Five checks, one message at a time.

The worked example below is illustrative — not a real incident. It shows how each section reads when filled in.

SECTION 01

Pick one message and follow it

Choose a specific failed message — sender, recipient, timestamp — and refuse to generalize until it is explained. Mail systems fail per-domain, per-recipient, and per-authentication-result; an 'all email' conclusion from one bounce is how afternoons disappear.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLETraced message: invoices to one customer domain, sent 09:14 CT. Other external mail flowed normally — direction and scope narrowed to one recipient domain. Not a real incident.
SECTION 02

Read the bounce properly

The bounce message is the receiving server's own testimony: the enhanced status code (5.7.x is authentication or policy, 5.1.x is addressing, 4.x is temporary) and the plain-language reason after it. Save the full bounce — headers included — before interpreting a summary of it.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEBounce code 5.7.26: DMARC failure reported by the receiving domain. The refusal was authentication policy, not reputation or content. Not a real incident.
SECTION 03

Trace the queue and relays

Follow the message through your own infrastructure: submission, queue, relay, and the receiving server's response to the connection. Where the trace stops is where the fault lives — a queue backing up is yours; a refusal at the far end is evidence to work with, not a mystery.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEThe message left the relay at 09:14:22 and was refused during the receiving handshake; queue empty, no internal backlog. The fault isolated to the receiving side's policy checks. Not a real incident.
SECTION 04

Verify SPF, DKIM, and DMARC

Authentication failures are configuration facts, checkable from outside: does SPF authorize the sending service, does DKIM sign and align, does the DMARC policy reject on failure? A new sending service added without an SPF update is the classic silent break.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEA billing platform added the previous week sent as the domain without appearing in SPF, and its DKIM did not align; DMARC at reject turned that into hard bounces. Not a real incident.
SECTION 05

Reputation and direction

If authentication is clean and one direction still fails, check reputation: blocklist status for the sending IPs and domain, and whether the failures correlate with volume or content changes. Separate 'they refuse us' from 'we cannot send' — the fixes live in different organizations.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLESending IP clean on major blocklists; the failures were DMARC-only for the new platform. Reputation cleared, the fix assigned to the authentication records. Not a real incident.

QUESTIONS

Email delivery, answered briefly.

What should I check first when email is not delivered?

Pick one specific failed message and get its full bounce. The enhanced status code and the receiving server's stated reason divide the problem immediately: 5.7.x means authentication or policy, 5.1.x means addressing, 4.x means a temporary condition. General checks come after that one message is explained.

How do SPF, DKIM, and DMARC affect email delivery?

SPF lists which servers may send for your domain, DKIM cryptographically signs messages, and DMARC tells receivers what to do when the first two fail — including rejecting the mail. A sending service missing from SPF, or signing with an unaligned domain, fails DMARC, and receivers with strict policies bounce mail that used to flow.

Why is email failing to only one domain?

Because receivers enforce policy independently. One domain may enforce DMARC rejection while others quarantine, or its gateway may block your IPs or content patterns specifically. Scope — one domain, one direction, one sender system — is evidence: it rules out your whole mail platform and focuses the trace.

No. The example on this page is illustrative — not a real incident from SolveStack or any customer. It shows how each section reads when filled in, with realistic-looking but invented details.
