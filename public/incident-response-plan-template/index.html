Incident Response Plan Template

An incident response plan template is the structure for deciding, before anything breaks, who leads an incident, who communicates, how severity is set, and when to escalate. Copy the blank plan, or read the scenario walkthrough. The record of a single incident belongs on the incident report template. The analysis belongs on the root cause analysis template.

01

WHAT THE PLAN DECIDES

Decided in advance, not improvised mid-incident.

An incident is the worst time to negotiate who is in charge. The plan settles the standing questions while everyone is calm: which role leads, how severity is declared, who writes the updates, and what happens when the first fix fails. Name a role for every line. A role survives a resignation; a person's phone number does not. The record of one incident goes on the incident report template. The tracked follow-up actions go on the corrective action plan template.

Six parts. The plan is done when each one names a role, not a hope.
Part What to write
Roles and responsibilities Who leads the incident, who communicates, who keeps the record — each a role, each with a backup.
Severity levels A short ladder of levels, each with a plain-language test anyone on call can apply in the first minutes.
Escalation path Who is pulled in next, in what order, and who can declare the incident over.
Communication cadence and channels Where updates are posted, who they are for, how often they land while an incident is open, and the format each update follows.
Response phases The path from detection to recovery, as a checklist the incident lead can walk without inventing the next step.
Post-incident review When the review happens, which records it reads, and how the changes it assigns get tracked — including changes to this plan.
PART 01

Roles and responsibilities

Three roles carry most incidents: the incident lead, who declares severity and directs the work; communications, who writes and posts updates; and the scribe, who keeps the timeline. Give every role a backup, and write down how the lead role passes when an incident crosses a shift or a specialty boundary.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEIncident lead: platform on-call. Communications: support lead. Scribe: second on-call. For identity incidents, the lead passes to the platform lead. Not a real incident.
PART 02

Severity levels

A severity level is a test, not a feeling. Write each level as a sentence a tired person can check at 3 a.m.: who is affected, and how badly. Pair each level with its response — who is paged and how often updates are posted — so declaring a severity also starts the right machinery.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLESEV-2: a customer-facing service is degraded, or all staff are locked out of internal tools. Response: page the incident lead and communications; updates every 30 minutes. Not a real incident.
PART 03

Escalation path

Escalation is a decision made in advance, not an admission of defeat. Name the order: who is pulled in when the first responder is stuck, who joins when the incident crosses teams, and the route for vendor incidents, where the escalation is a support case rather than a person. End the path with who can declare the incident over.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEStuck after 30 minutes: page the service owner. Cross-team impact: the incident lead pulls in the second team's on-call. Vendor fault: open the priority support case and keep the update cadence. Not a real incident.
PART 04

Communication cadence and channels

Silence is what turns an incident into a rumor. Name one status channel, the audience for each kind of notice, and a cadence per severity. Give updates a fixed shape — what is broken, what is being done, when the next update lands — so a reader can scan any update in seconds.

PART 05

Response phases

Six phases are enough: detect, declare, contain, resolve, recover, review. Contain and resolve are different jobs — stop the impact spreading first, then fix the cause. Recover is its own step: service is normal when the checks say so, not when the fix is deployed. The review phase is part of the response, not an optional extra.

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLEContain: route traffic away from the failing region. Resolve: fix the bad deploy. Recover: watch the error rate for one full update cycle before closing. Not a real incident.
PART 06

Post-incident review

The review reads the incident report and the root cause analysis, assigns the corrective actions, and — the step most plans miss — records what the incident exposed about the plan itself. A severity test that did not fit, a role nobody filled, a channel nobody watched: those are plan defects, and they get actions like any other.

QUESTIONS

Incident response plans, answered briefly.

What is an incident response plan template?

An incident response plan template is the fill-in structure for a team's incident response plan: the roles, severity levels, escalation path, communication cadence, and response phases decided before an incident. The plan says who does what. The incident report records what one incident actually did.

What should an incident response plan include?

Include named roles with backups, severity levels with a plain-language test for each one, an escalation path with contact order, the channels and cadence for updates, the response phases from detection to recovery, and a post-incident review step that feeds changes back into the plan.

How is an incident response plan different from an incident report?

The plan is written before anything breaks and applies to every incident: who leads, who communicates, when to escalate. The incident report is written during and after one incident and records its summary, timeline, impact, and actions. The plan governs; the report remembers.

How often should an incident response plan be reviewed?

Review it after every significant incident, while the gaps are still fresh, and whenever the team, the on-call rotation, or the systems it covers change. A plan that names people who left or tools nobody uses fails the first time it is needed.

Can I keep an incident response plan offline?

Yes. The template on this page is plain text you can copy into any document or wiki. SolveStack, the app linked from this page, also works offline: investigations stay in your browser, with no account and no cloud service, and export as Markdown, HTML, JSON, or ZIP.
